  • The Mysterious Benedict Society, by Trenton Lee Stewart

    I think the best children’s books leave those who read them with some sort of idea or principle, and The Mysterious Benedict Society does exactly that. In particular, you end up believing that (a) even the most exemplary people have weaknesses, (b) you are responsible for the sort of person you become, and (c) communities are better when they are made up of individuals.

    1. Mr. Benedict, the genius, has narcolepsy. All-rounder Reynie constantly wrestles with loneliness and self-doubt, the mentalist Sticky with crippling anxiety, and the gymnast Kate with accepting help. Constance is precocious and wonderfully bad-tempered.
    2. Identical twins Mr. Benedict and Mr. Curtain are polar opposites in terms of personality.
    3. Despite all of its inner turmoil, the Mysterious Benedict Society is just a better community than that of the Learning Institute. For one, they’re more adaptable—members with specific strengths assign themselves to the tasks that suit them, so they naturally accomplish more. The Learning Institute, on the other hand, assembles its students into a colorless, structured mass, and can't adapt to anything. For another, they won!

    These are fairly important values for a child to grow up with. Without (a), you might fall into perfectionist patterns, and perfectionism—attempting to address your flaws—is generally less productive than refining your strengths, which I would call pursuing individuality. Moreover, without (b), you might never find the motivation to do that. In my opinion, (c) is meant to oppose the natural tendency to “fit in,” which is itself at odds with individuality, and when taken together, (b) and (c) show that one should leap into themselves, as it were. Also, the Mysterious Benedict Society's weaknesses aren't presented as inherent to their respective member, but instead as temporary failings that can be overcome through a concentrated effort. Along with (b), this shows a child that their future is under their control.

    In short, beyond being a fun read, this book guides children (and adults, too!) to find and do what they are good at. While this might be a natural sentiment for many, it certainly wasn't for me.
